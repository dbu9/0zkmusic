Electronic music is often described as the sound of the future. In the work of Eduard Artemyev, however, electronics could sound ancient, wounded and strangely familiar. His synthesizers did not merely represent spaceships, laboratories or technological progress. They remembered forests, churches, childhood, war and vanished civilizations. They transformed natural sound into something supernatural without completely separating it from the Earth.

This is what distinguishes Artemyev from many electronic pioneers of the twentieth century. He did not treat the machine as the opposite of humanity. He used it to search for the hidden life inside human memory.

Born in Novosibirsk in 1937, Artemyev received a traditional education in composition at the Moscow Conservatory. Soon after graduating in 1960, he encountered engineer Evgeny Murzin and his extraordinary ANS synthesizer. At the time, electronic music was still a marginal and uncertain territory. The synthesizer was not yet a familiar keyboard instrument, and there was no established profession called “electronic musician.” Artemyev entered the field before its rules had been written.

## The instrument that could draw sound

The ANS was unlike the commercial synthesizers that would later appear in Western studios. It had no normal musical keyboard. Its interface was a glass plate covered with an opaque material. The composer scratched lines, points and shapes into this surface, allowing light to pass through the drawing. Photoelectric cells then translated the image into sound.

The machine offered 720 microtonal pitches across ten octaves. A composer could create enormous clusters, glissandi, clouds of noise and harmonic structures that would have been almost impossible for a conventional orchestra to perform. Pitch, duration, density and timbre became parts of a visual design. Music could literally be drawn.

Artemyev did not invent the ANS, but he became one of the musicians who revealed what such an instrument could mean artistically. His conservatory training gave him a strong understanding of harmony, counterpoint and large musical form, while the ANS allowed him to escape the limitations of conventional notation and equal temperament. He could compose not only with notes, but with the internal spectrum of sound itself. The ANS produced 720 available microtones in intervals of approximately 16.67 cents—far smaller than a conventional semitone. [East European Film Bulletin](https://eefb.org/retrospectives/eduard-artemyevs-compositional-strategies/?pdf=7883)

This was one of Artemyev’s most important innovations: he treated timbre, acoustic density and the transition between noise and harmony as compositional material. Today, these ideas are familiar in ambient music, drone, spectral composition and cinematic sound design. In the Soviet Union of the early 1960s, they were radical.

## *Solaris*: electronic music without the mythology of progress

Artemyev’s international reputation rests heavily on his collaboration with Andrei Tarkovsky. Their first film together, *Solaris*, appeared in 1972.

Tarkovsky did not want a conventional science-fiction soundtrack. He was suspicious of music that instructed the audience when to feel fear, sorrow or wonder. What he wanted was something closer to an organized acoustic environment: natural sounds, mechanical noises and barely perceptible musical structures merging into one another.

Artemyev responded by creating a language in which the difference between music and sound design became uncertain. The hum of the space station, metallic resonances, distant voices and electronic clusters seem to belong to the physical world of the film, yet they also express the characters’ interior states.

The contrast at the heart of *Solaris* is not simply between Earth and space. It is between human memory and an intelligence that cannot be understood in human terms. Bach’s chorale prelude *Ich ruf zu dir, Herr Jesu Christ* represents the fragile continuity of earthly culture. Artemyev surrounds and transforms it with the ANS, electronic textures and altered instrumental colors. Bach does not appear as a museum object. His music seems to be remembered from an impossible distance, as if the machine itself were trying to reconstruct humanity.

This was far removed from the optimistic electronic futurism of the period. Artemyev did not make space sound clean, rational or triumphant. His electronic cosmos was lonely, organic and psychologically unstable. The technology in *Solaris* does not liberate humanity from the past. It carries the past into space, where it returns in distorted form.

The music was initially inseparable from Tarkovsky’s images and for decades was difficult to obtain as an independent soundtrack. Its later vinyl reissues helped listeners encounter it as a major electronic work in its own right, rather than merely an element of a famous film. [Under the Radar](https://www.undertheradarmag.com/blog/reissued_and_revisited_eduard_artemievs_solaris_original_soundtrack/)

## From music to acoustic reality

Artemyev continued working with Tarkovsky on *Mirror* and *Stalker*. In these films, his method became even less dependent on traditional melody.

In *Mirror*, natural and electronic sounds continually exchange identities. Fire becomes a low electronic drone. Water appears artificial. Orchestral chords grow out of noise and dissolve back into it. A sound may begin as part of the environment and end as music, or begin as an instrumental tone and become indistinguishable from wind, machinery or memory.

This approach now seems modern because contemporary cinema routinely combines composition, field recording and sound design. Artemyev was doing it when those activities were usually treated as separate professions.

His innovation was not simply the use of a synthesizer. It was the decision to transform the entire audible world into an instrument.

## *Stalker* and the meeting of worlds

For *Stalker*, Artemyev worked with the much larger EMS Synthi 100 system. Once again, he avoided the obvious vocabulary of science fiction. The central musical material combines electronic drones with the sounds of flute, tar and colors resembling the Indian tanpura. Acoustic performances were filtered, slowed, reverberated and passed through the synthesizer until their cultural and physical origins became uncertain.

The result is neither fully electronic nor fully acoustic, neither clearly European nor clearly Asian. This was intentional. Tarkovsky had evoked the meeting of East and West, and Artemyev attempted to create a musical space in which the two could no longer be separated.

He manipulated tape speed, filtering, delay and modulation not as decorative effects but as part of the composition. The train rhythms in *Stalker* become mechanical counterpoint. Water drops form patterns that seem almost—but not quite—natural. Voices turn into spectral choirs. The Zone acquires its own acoustic physics.

In modern terminology, Artemyev was creating hybrid sound objects. A listener might recognize the trace of an instrument without being able to identify the final sound. This uncertainty is essential to the film: the Zone is terrifying not because it is visibly alien, but because familiar reality has begun to obey unknown laws.

## Beyond Tarkovsky

It would be misleading to define Artemyev only through his three films with Tarkovsky. He composed music for more than 150 films and worked extensively with Andrei Konchalovsky and Nikita Mikhalkov. These collaborations revealed another side of his personality.

Where Tarkovsky encouraged ambiguity and restraint, Konchalovsky and Mikhalkov often gave Artemyev space for broad melody, historical drama and emotional directness. Scores for *At Home Among Strangers*, *Siberiade*, *A Slave of Love*, *Burnt by the Sun* and *The Barber of Siberia* demonstrate his ability to move between electronic experimentation, symphonic writing, Russian lyricism, folk intonation and almost rock-like rhythmic power.

The theme from *Siberiade* is perhaps the clearest example. Its rising melody feels heroic, tragic and cosmic at the same time. It is simple enough to be remembered immediately, but its electronic orchestration places it outside any definite historical period. It might accompany industrial conquest, the exploration of space or the funeral of an entire civilization.

More than two decades later, the Russian group PPK adapted this theme for the trance single “ResuRection.” The track reached number three on the UK Singles Chart in 2001. A melody written for a Soviet historical epic had crossed into the international club culture of the new century without losing its identity. [Official Charts](https://www.officialcharts.com/songs/ppk-resurection/)

This episode says something important about Artemyev’s music. Beneath its sophisticated production and philosophical context, it often contained powerful melodic architecture. Remove the film, change the tempo and replace the orchestration, and the underlying idea could still survive.

## A composer between institutions

Artemyev’s public acceptance during his lifetime was unusual because it occurred on several different levels.

Experimental electronic composition remained a specialized field in the Soviet Union. Many listeners who knew Artemyev’s music through films did not necessarily think of him as an electronic avant-garde composer. They remembered his themes, atmospheres and songs. Cinema allowed his experiments to reach millions of people who would never have attended a concert of electroacoustic music.

Among filmmakers and composers, however, his importance was clearly recognized. He became one of the central figures of Soviet and Russian film music, helped establish the Russian Association of Electroacoustic Music and remained its president for many years. His honors included four State Prizes, the title of People’s Artist of Russia and, shortly before his death, Hero of Labour of the Russian Federation. His music was used during both the opening and closing ceremonies of the 2014 Winter Olympics in Sochi.

Therefore, Artemyev was not an ignored genius discovered only after death. He was highly respected and, through cinema, widely heard during his lifetime. But the different parts of his achievement were not always perceived as a single whole. The general public knew the melodies. International cinephiles knew the Tarkovsky soundscapes. Electronic musicians knew the ANS experiments. Only gradually did these identities begin to merge.

## Recognition after his death

Eduard Artemyev died in Moscow on 29 December 2022, aged 85. By then, he had received the highest levels of official recognition in Russia, while his electronic work had acquired an international cult reputation. [RIA Novosti](https://ria.ru/20221229/artemev-1842290287.html)

His posthumous reputation is therefore not a simple story of rediscovery. It is better understood as reclassification. Artemyev is increasingly heard not only as a great Soviet film composer, but as one of the musicians who changed the relationship between composition, technology and recorded reality.

His work anticipated several directions that later became central to electronic music: ambient soundscapes, drone, microtonality, spectral transformation, hybrid acoustic-electronic production and the dissolution of the border between music and environmental sound. Yet his importance cannot be reduced to a list of techniques.

Artemyev understood that a new instrument does not become meaningful merely because it produces a new sound. It becomes meaningful when that sound is connected to memory, mortality and human experience.

This is why his music has aged so well. Many early electronic works remain historically interesting but emotionally tied to the novelty of their equipment. Artemyev’s finest music no longer sounds like a demonstration of old technology. The machines have become obsolete, but the world he created through them has not.

In *Solaris*, *Mirror* and *Stalker*, electronics do not announce the arrival of the future. They reveal that the future will still be haunted by Bach, childhood, faith, fear and the sound of wind moving through grass.

For Artemyev, the synthesizer was never merely a machine. It was a place where the human and the inhuman could listen to one another.
